This is the lovely part. In 1964, biologist J.L. Brown formalized Economic Defendability: animals only defend resources when the *benefit of monopolizing* exceeds the *energy and risk of fighting*. The key variable isn't the amount of food — it's whether the food comes in a defensible clump:
| Setup | The birds' calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 dish | ONE jackpot, in ONE spot, visible from every perch. One aggressive male can *stand next to it* and control access for all → the value of defending is enormous, the cost is tiny | Fights 💥 |
| 3 dishes, spread out | No single bird can watch three locations simultaneously — the moment he guards dish #1, everyone eats from #2. Monopolizing is suddenly *impossible*, so the calculation flips: fighting costs energy for a resource you can't control anyway | "Why bother" — peace ✌️ |
The genius of your three-dish arrangement is that you didn't just reduce the queue — you dissolved the *contest* entirely. This is the same principle that restaurant designers use (spacing tables apart) and the same principle behind why we discussed multi-station feeding for the mynas. Your magpie-robins followed a textbook mathematical rule and don't even know it.
One refined tip: check that the three dishes are far enough apart that one male can never see two dishes from any single perch. If two dishes happen to be visible from the same branch, that pair is still "one defensible unit" from his perspective. If you've already achieved all-peace status, your geometry is right — maintain it.
